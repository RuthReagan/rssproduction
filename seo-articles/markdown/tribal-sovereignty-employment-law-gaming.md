# Tribal Sovereignty and Employment Law: What Every Tribal Gaming Employer Must Understand

_Tribal sovereignty shapes employment law for gaming operations. Learn how tribal employers navigate jurisdiction, Indian Preference, and HR compliance._

Tribal sovereignty is not a legal technicality. It is the foundation on which every employment decision at a tribal gaming enterprise rests, and misunderstanding it creates real liability. Tribal gaming employers operate in a legal environment that is distinct from state and federal employment law in ways that most HR professionals, and many attorneys, have never been trained to navigate. Getting this right requires more than good intentions. It requires a clear-eyed understanding of how sovereignty shapes your obligations, your protections, and your exposure.

## Sovereignty Is Not an Exemption. It Is a Jurisdiction.

One of the most common mistakes I see when tribal gaming operations bring in outside HR consultants is the assumption that tribal sovereignty means the tribe is exempt from employment law. That framing is wrong, and it leads to bad decisions. Sovereignty means the tribe has its own jurisdiction, its own legal authority, and in many cases its own employment laws. The question is never simply 'does federal law apply?' The question is which law governs this employment relationship, and what does that law actually require.

For tribal gaming operations, the answer depends on several factors: whether the employee is tribal or non-tribal, whether the work occurs on trust land, what the tribe's own employment ordinances say, and what waivers of sovereign immunity, if any, the tribe has adopted. A non-tribal employee working on tribal trust land in a gaming facility may have very different legal recourse than a state employee in the same role. That distinction matters enormously when you are designing your HR policies, your grievance procedures, and your termination processes.

Tribal HR leaders and gaming executives need to stop treating sovereignty as a shield and start treating it as a framework. The tribes that get this right build employment systems that are legally sound, culturally grounded, and defensible. The ones that get it wrong end up in federal court arguing positions they cannot win, or they create internal cultures where employees feel they have no recourse and no voice.

## Which Federal Employment Laws Apply to Tribal Employers?

The short answer is: more than most people think, but not all of them. Title VII of the Civil Rights Act, the Americans with Disabilities Act, the Age Discrimination in Employment Act, and the Fair Labor Standards Act all apply to tribal employers in most circumstances, particularly when the tribe employs a significant number of non-tribal members. The Indian Reorganization Act and the Indian Self-Determination Act carve out specific spaces for tribal preference, but they do not eliminate the tribe's obligations under federal anti-discrimination law for the broader workforce.

The Family and Medical Leave Act applies to tribal employers who meet the employee threshold, just as it does to any other covered employer. OSHA jurisdiction over tribal gaming operations has been litigated extensively, and the current consensus is that OSHA applies to tribal gaming workplaces, particularly those operating under NIGC oversight. Workers' compensation is a more complex area: some tribes have enacted their own workers' compensation codes, while others have entered into state compacts that extend state coverage to tribal employees.

What does not apply, in most cases, is state employment law. Oklahoma's anti-discrimination statutes, its wage payment laws, and its unemployment insurance system generally do not reach employees working on tribal trust land. This creates a gap that tribal employers must fill deliberately. If you are not covered by state law and you have not enacted your own tribal employment ordinance, your employees may have fewer protections than they would working for any other employer in the state. That is not a position any responsible tribal government should want to be in.

## Building an Employment Ordinance That Actually Works

Every tribal gaming operation should have a tribal employment ordinance, and most do. The problem is that many of those ordinances were drafted years ago, have not been updated since, and do not reflect the current scale or complexity of the gaming operation. An ordinance written when the casino had 200 employees may be wholly inadequate for an operation with 1,200 employees, multiple properties, and a workforce that includes a significant percentage of non-tribal members.

A functional tribal employment ordinance needs to address at minimum: the scope of covered employees, the tribe's Indian Preference policy and how it is applied, the grievance and appeals process, anti-discrimination protections, wage and hour standards, leave policies, and the process for termination. It should also address the limited waiver of sovereign immunity that allows employees to bring internal grievances, and it should be clear about what forum has jurisdiction over employment disputes. Vague ordinances invite litigation and create inconsistent application.

I have worked with tribal gaming operations where the employment ordinance was so outdated that it referenced positions that no longer existed and omitted entire departments that had been created in the intervening decade. Updating the ordinance is not a bureaucratic exercise. It is a risk management priority. When an employee files a complaint, the first thing any investigator or tribunal will look at is whether the tribe had a clear, current, and consistently applied employment policy. If the answer is no, the tribe's legal position weakens considerably.

## Indian Preference in Gaming: Applying It Correctly and Defensibly

Indian Preference is one of the most powerful tools a tribal gaming operation has for building a workforce that reflects the community it serves. It is also one of the most frequently misapplied. Indian Preference is not a quota system. It is a preference in hiring and promotion that gives qualified tribal members and, in many cases, members of other federally recognized tribes, priority consideration over equally or less qualified non-tribal candidates. The key word is qualified. Preference does not mean hiring an unqualified candidate over a qualified one. It means that when qualifications are comparable, tribal membership is a deciding factor.

The legal basis for Indian Preference in tribal employment is well established. Morton v. Mancari, decided by the Supreme Court in 1974, held that Indian Preference in federal employment is a political classification, not a racial one, and is therefore not subject to the same constitutional scrutiny as race-based preferences. That reasoning has been extended to tribal employer preference policies in most federal circuits. But the preference must be tied to tribal membership, not to race or ancestry alone, and it must be applied consistently.

Where I see tribal gaming operations get into trouble is in the documentation. If you cannot show, in writing, that you applied your Indian Preference policy consistently, that you evaluated candidates against the same criteria, and that tribal preference was applied at the appropriate stage of the selection process, you are exposed. A non-tribal candidate who was passed over for a less experienced tribal member can challenge that decision if the process was not documented. Build the documentation into your hiring workflow, not as an afterthought.

## Sovereign Immunity and the Employee Grievance Process

Sovereign immunity protects tribal governments from lawsuits in state and federal court without the tribe's consent. In the employment context, this means that a tribal employee who believes they were wrongfully terminated or discriminated against generally cannot sue the tribe in state court. This protection is real and it is significant. But it creates a corresponding obligation: if employees cannot access external courts, the tribe must provide a meaningful internal process for resolving employment disputes.

A grievance process that exists on paper but is not actually accessible, impartial, or timely is not a meaningful process. I have seen tribal gaming operations where the grievance procedure required employees to appeal to the same manager who made the termination decision, with no independent review. That is not a grievance process. It is a formality that will not withstand scrutiny if the matter ever reaches a federal forum. The NLRB, the EEOC, and federal courts have all weighed in on what constitutes adequate tribal employment dispute resolution, and the bar is higher than many tribes realize.

The best tribal gaming operations I have worked with have established independent employment tribunals or hearing officers who are not in the direct chain of command of the department involved in the dispute. They have clear timelines, written decisions, and an appeals path that goes to tribal council or a designated appeals body. This structure protects the tribe legally and builds employee trust. When people know they have a fair process, they are more likely to use it and less likely to seek external remedies.

## Navigating NLRA Jurisdiction in Tribal Gaming

The National Labor Relations Act and its application to tribal gaming employers has been one of the most actively litigated areas of tribal employment law over the past two decades. The NLRB has asserted jurisdiction over tribal gaming operations in several cases, arguing that the commerce clause gives it authority over enterprises that substantially affect interstate commerce. Tribal gaming operations, by their nature, meet that threshold. The question of whether the NLRA applies to tribal employers is not settled law, and the answer may depend on the specific facts of your operation and the circuit in which you operate.

What this means practically is that tribal gaming employers need to be prepared for the possibility of union organizing activity and NLRB proceedings, even if they believe the NLRA does not apply to them. Asserting sovereign immunity as a defense to NLRB jurisdiction is a legitimate legal strategy, but it is not a guaranteed win. Several tribal gaming operations have litigated this issue and lost at the administrative level, even when they ultimately prevailed in federal court. The litigation is expensive and disruptive.

The better approach is to build a workplace where employees do not feel the need to organize. That means competitive wages, consistent and fair treatment, accessible management, and a grievance process that works. I am not suggesting that unions are inherently bad for tribal gaming operations. I am saying that the best defense against unwanted organizing is a workplace where employees feel heard and treated fairly. That is an HR strategy, not a legal one, and it is far less expensive than years of NLRB litigation.

## Practical Steps for Tribal Gaming HR Leaders

If you are the HR director or CHRO of a tribal gaming operation, there are several concrete actions that will reduce your legal exposure and strengthen your employment practices. Start with a comprehensive review of your tribal employment ordinance. When was it last updated? Does it reflect your current workforce size, your current property footprint, and your current Indian Preference policy? If it has not been reviewed in the past three years, it needs attention now.

Next, audit your hiring documentation. Can you demonstrate, for every hire in the past two years, that your Indian Preference policy was applied consistently and documented at each stage? If not, build that documentation into your applicant tracking system or your hiring workflow immediately. The cost of retrofitting documentation after a complaint is filed is far higher than the cost of building it in from the start.

Finally, assess your grievance process honestly. Ask yourself: if I were an employee who believed I had been treated unfairly, would I trust this process? Would I believe it was impartial? Would I know how to use it? If the answer to any of those questions is no, the process needs to be redesigned. Tribal sovereignty gives your organization significant legal protections. It also creates a corresponding responsibility to treat your employees with the same fairness and rigor that any good employer owes its workforce.

## When to Bring in Outside Expertise

Tribal employment law is a specialty. Most employment attorneys, even experienced ones, have limited exposure to the intersection of tribal sovereignty, NIGC regulations, and federal employment law. When your gaming operation is facing a significant employment dispute, a union organizing campaign, or a major policy overhaul, you need advisors who have worked specifically in the tribal gaming context, not generalists who are learning on your dime.

The same applies to HR consulting. A consultant who has spent their career in corporate HR or state government HR will bring assumptions about the legal framework that simply do not apply in a tribal gaming environment. They may not know that your state's anti-discrimination statute does not apply to your workforce, or that your Indian Preference policy needs to be structured as a political preference rather than a racial one to survive legal challenge. These are not minor details. They are foundational to getting your HR function right.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does Title VII apply to tribal gaming employers?

Yes, in most cases. Title VII applies to tribal employers who employ 15 or more employees and engage in commerce, which virtually all tribal gaming operations do. The Indian Preference exception allows tribes to prefer tribal members in hiring and promotion, but it does not exempt the tribe from Title VII's prohibitions on discrimination based on race, sex, religion, or national origin for the broader workforce.

### Can a tribal gaming employee sue the tribe for wrongful termination?

Generally, sovereign immunity protects the tribe from lawsuits in state or federal court without the tribe's consent. However, if the tribe has enacted a limited waiver of sovereign immunity in its employment ordinance, or if the employee can establish a federal statutory claim that overrides immunity, litigation is possible. This is why a robust internal grievance process is essential: it provides employees a meaningful remedy without requiring the tribe to waive immunity broadly.

### What is the difference between Indian Preference and affirmative action?

Indian Preference is a hiring and promotion preference for members of federally recognized tribes, grounded in the political relationship between the federal government and tribal nations, not in race. The Supreme Court upheld this distinction in Morton v. Mancari (1974). Affirmative action, by contrast, is a race-conscious remedy for historical discrimination and is subject to strict constitutional scrutiny. Indian Preference, properly structured and documented, is legally distinct and does not require the same justification.

### How often should a tribal gaming operation update its employment ordinance?

At minimum, every three years, and any time there is a significant change in the operation: a new property opening, a major workforce expansion, a change in tribal leadership, or a new federal ruling affecting tribal employment law. The ordinance should also be reviewed whenever the gaming compact with the state is renegotiated, as compact terms can affect employment-related obligations.
